\begin{abstract}
What does the aggregate MMLU score actually measure? We answer the question psychometrically. Item difficulty is calibrated with a 2PL IRT model fitted to 1,000 open-weights language models over all 14,042 MMLU test items, and difficulty is then regressed on a deterministic, text-extractable framework of five dimensions and nine indicators: topological reasoning load, syntactic dependency structure, epistemological specificity, semantic abstractness, and adversarial negation scope. The mapping from structural complexity to difficulty is then tested for invariance across MMLU's own STEM and non-STEM partitions.

Invariance is rejected decisively. A joint Wald test built on heteroskedasticity-consistent covariances gives $W(9) = 138.36$, $p = 2.3 \times 10^{-25}$, and a 10,000-draw label permutation places the observed between-domain divergence beyond every draw of its own null ($p < 10^{-4}$). Structural complexity explains roughly twice as much cross-validated difficulty variance in STEM as outside it ($R^{2}_{\mathrm{cv}} = 0.073$ against $0.036$), and between $2.5$ and $3.8$ times as much as a word-count-plus-readability baseline. The divergence replicates in 40 of 40 random half-samples and survives refitting difficulty under a Rasch model.

The consequences are practical. Aggregate leaderboard rank tracks non-STEM accuracy more closely than STEM accuracy (weighted Kendall $\tau_{w} = 0.9887$ against $0.9634$), so selecting a Top-50 model on the aggregate for a reasoning-intensive deployment displaces 22\% of the STEM-appropriate choices, and higher-ability models degrade more steeply under reasoning depth once floor-constrained models are removed ($r = -0.40$ to $-0.51$; $-0.77$ in the uncorrected full sample). The MMLU aggregate weights retrieval capacity and reasoning stability unequally. We release the framework as a reproducible auditing instrument and recommend disaggregated reporting.
\end{abstract}
