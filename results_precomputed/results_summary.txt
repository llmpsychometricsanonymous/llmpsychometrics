EXPLAINED VARIANCE IN ABSOLUTE UNITS

R-squared is a ratio, so a partition with a wider spread of difficulty can
post a higher R-squared at equal predictive accuracy. Reporting the
explained variance itself, in logit-squared units, removes that scaling.

------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM           3153   2.3772   5.6509   2.2843     0.4327   0.0792
non-STEM      10889   2.1740   4.7262   2.1332     0.1758   0.0380

  STEM / non-STEM ratio, R-squared            2.08x   95% CI [1.52, 2.80]
  STEM / non-STEM ratio, explained variance   2.46x   95% CI [1.80, 3.36]

The contrast is not an artefact of the wider STEM difficulty distribution.
Measured in logit-squared units the structural indicators account for
0.4327 of STEM difficulty variance against 0.1758 outside it,
a ratio of 2.46x, larger than the 2.08x implied by the R-squared comparison.

READING

Option-set structure alone cross-validates at 0.0684 pooled, against
0.0338 for the nine stem indicators; together they reach 0.0808. The
two sources of difficulty are largely complementary rather than redundant,
which is what the construction of a multiple-choice item implies: the stem
fixes what must be worked out and the option set fixes how finely the
answer must be discriminated.

This also diagnoses the HellaSwag transfer result in stage 40. There the
stem-only framework explains essentially nothing within a source (0.0037,
0.0035) because HellaSwag's difficulty was manufactured by adversarially
filtering the endings. A stem-only instrument is blind to that by
construction, and the correct reading of that null is a scope condition on
the instrument rather than a failure of the finding it was built to test.

THE FRAMEWORK AS A FRACTION OF THE CEILING

Two denominators are reported. The encoder ceiling is the best purely
text-based predictor; the encoder-plus-subject ceiling adds topic
membership, which is recoverable from the text in principle and is the
more conservative comparison. Percentages against the smaller denominator
should be read with the caveat that ridge over 384 dimensions is estimated
on fewer items inside a partition than pooled, so the ceiling is itself
less precisely determined in STEM than it is overall.

READING

A dense encoder reading the whole item reaches 0.1269 pooled,
0.1330 in STEM. Most difficulty is therefore not recoverable from
the item text by any method: the ceiling is low in absolute terms, which
is the honest context for the framework's own share and cannot be read off
an R-squared against total variance.

Subject identity alone reaches 0.1354 pooled. A large part of what
an encoder knows about an MMLU item is which subject it came from, which
is topic membership rather than structural complexity. The nine indicators
are not a proxy for topic: they are computed from text structure and carry
no subject label.

The comparison worth reporting is the framework against the ceiling, not
against 1.0. Against a ceiling estimated the same way on the same folds,
the nine indicators recover 57% of text-predictable difficulty in STEM and
33% outside it, while remaining interpretable and theory-grounded, which
a 384-dimensional embedding is not.

Adding the option indicators takes that to 90% of the encoder ceiling in STEM
(75% against the conservative encoder-plus-subject ceiling). Seventeen
interpretable features recover most of what a dense representation of
the same item can extract. The residual gap is the part of difficulty
that is neither structural nor topical, and on this evidence it is
small relative to the part that is simply not in the text at all.

READING

This is the invariance test the psychometric literature the paper cites is
actually about, and it is a different question from the one the rest of the
paper asks. Item-level DIF here means that two models of equal estimated
ability but different architecture lineage have systematically different
odds of answering particular items correctly. Where that is widespread, a
single ability estimate is not comparable across families, and leaderboard
positions that mix families are comparing scores that the measurement model
does not licence comparing.

Two qualifications. Mantel-Haenszel matches on a single ability estimate,
so if the latent space is genuinely multidimensional -- which is what the
rest of this paper argues -- some of what is flagged here is that
multidimensionality reappearing as DIF rather than an independent defect.
The two findings are symptoms of one cause, and should be reported as
such rather than as separate evidence. Second, family labels are read off
model names, and a large part of the population consists of merges and
fine-tunes whose lineage is mixed; the reference and focal groups are
therefore noisier than examinee groups in an educational setting.

WHY THIS IS NOT REPORTED IN THE PAPER

First, sigma_e = 4.93 logits exceeds the standard deviation of the
difficulty distribution the two-stage calibration estimates (2.24). A
residual item term larger than the quantity it is a residual of is not a
plausible parameter value. Second, the credible intervals above are
roughly an order of magnitude narrower than the HC3 standard errors on
the same contrasts from the two-stage fit, which is the signature of a
mean-field variational posterior collapsing rather than of a sharper
estimate. Third, several interaction signs disagree with the two-stage
OLS, and the convergence check below shows those signs are not stable.

Same model on a 250-model subsample at increasing step counts.
A converged fit is stable across them.

   steps   sigma_e   sigma_u      d_MDD    d_Nodes  d_Density    d_Depth
------------------------------------------------------------------------
    3000     2.600     2.105     -0.007     -0.452      0.091      0.767
    8000     5.211     2.051      0.337     -0.153      0.535      0.471
   20000     5.383     1.835      0.380      1.144      0.454     -0.022

The parameters move with the step budget and two interaction
coefficients change sign, so the optimisation has not found a stable
solution. The model is weakly identified as written: a free per-item
residual over 14,042 items can absorb whatever the indicators would
otherwise explain, leaving beta and delta poorly constrained, and a
mean-field guide handles that badly.

We therefore report the two-stage analysis as the paper's inference and
leave the joint explanatory model as future work. Making it usable needs
a tighter identification strategy -- a strict LLTM without the free item
residual, or an informative prior on its scale -- together with an
inference method whose posterior spread can be trusted. Reporting the
unstable fit because 9 of 9 intervals happen to exclude zero would be
reporting an artefact of the optimiser.

READING

The two nulls answer different questions and give different
answers.

Slope non-invariance survives. The LRT statistic is beyond
the subject-block null at p = 0.0488, and the
clustered Wald test rejects equality at W(9) = 32.22,
p = 1.82e-04. Clustering inflates the slope standard
errors by about 1.50x and the statistic falls from
138.36 to 32.22, but the conclusion does not
depend on treating items as independent.

The R-squared gap does not survive. Against the item-level
null the gap is extreme (p = 0.0001); against the
subject-block null it is ordinary (p = 0.0804).
The block null is 3.2x wider, because a random
union of whole subjects inherits the between-subject
variation in how predictable difficulty is, and the
item-level null destroys exactly that variation. A gap of
0.0412 is what any 19-subject grouping of
MMLU tends to produce. It is therefore not evidence that
the STEM / non-STEM boundary specifically marks a change in
how well structural complexity predicts difficulty.

What the gap does remain is a description of these two
partitions, which is what a practitioner reading the
leaderboard actually faces. It is not a rejected null.

indicators on length leaves the domain contrast intact.

The published slope is marginal, and WSCG Depth is correlated with

whether depth matters beyond how hard the item turned out to be,
which conditions on a consequence of depth and should be read as a
bound rather than as the estimate.

THE INTERACTION ESTIMATED INSIDE THE RESPONSE MODEL

logit P(Y_ji = 1) = a_i (theta_j - b_i) + (g0 + g1 theta_j) W_i,
with the lower asymptote fixed at the guessing rate and WSCG Depth
standardised. This estimates the interaction in one fit, respects
the floor natively, and does not read theta and the slope off
overlapping data.

  gamma0 (complexity effect at mean ability) : -0.3001  [-0.3263, -0.2715]
  gamma1 (ability x complexity)              : -0.0859  [-0.0926, -0.0794]
  95% credible interval excludes zero        : yes
  final ELBO loss                            : 1380071.1

Mean-field variational intervals understate posterior spread, so
the interval above is a lower bound on uncertainty and the sign
and magnitude of gamma1 are what should be read from it.


READING

The floor correction the paper applies is not sufficient on its
own terms. Trimming removes models whose slopes are flat by
constraint; it does not remove the compression that operates
continuously above the trim point, and the simulated null
reproduces a substantial share of the published correlation at
every trim level.

A residual of r = -0.278 survives at the trim the paper
reports, so the effect is not wholly mechanical, but the
published figure is not the size of the effect and should not
be quoted as though it were.

The model-based estimate is the one to quote, because it does not
depend on the two-stage geometry at all. It is reported above with
its sign and interval, and Section 5.1 has been rewritten around
it rather than around the marginal correlation.

READING

The interaction reparameterisation makes the design one model
and one test, and the estimator ladder shows exactly what the
independence assumption was buying. Items are not independent
draws, and correcting for that costs the joint test most of its
apparent force: W(9) falls from 138 to 28, standard
errors inflate by a median 1.65x, and against a
bootstrap reference the joint p is 0.060 rather than 2e-25.

At the joint level the honest statement is that the
rejection is marginal once subject clustering is respected
and a bootstrap reference is used: p = 0.060, not
significant at the conventional threshold. We report it that
way. What does not depend on the joint test is the
individual contrast that survives every correction applied
here, including Holm over the family of nine:
  Entity Density
The claim the paper can support is therefore specific rather
than omnibus -- named indicators whose contribution to
difficulty is domain-dependent -- and it is supported
independently by the confirmatory two-dimensional IRT fit,
which does not use these standard errors at all.

Standardising within partitions does not dissolve the contrasts,
and it does not manufacture them either: the same indicators
separate on both scales. Reporting the standardised column
removes a units objection that the raw column cannot answer.

READING

On common support the contrasts hold. Restricting both
partitions to the range of the predictor they share changes no
sign and no conclusion for Syntactic MDD, Entity Density, Lexical Concreteness,
so none of them is an artefact of the two domains occupying
different regions of the predictor.

Linearity is a different matter, and it does not hold uniformly.
A four-knot spline improves on the straight line in Syntactic MDD (non-STEM), Lexical Concreteness (non-STEM),
all of them outside STEM. The fitted curves say what the
linear coefficients cannot: in STEM both Syntactic MDD and
Entity Density rise monotonically with difficulty across the
whole common support, while outside STEM each traces a shallow
hump that a single slope averages to near zero. The domain
difference is therefore between a monotone relationship and a
non-monotone one, which is a sharper statement than a
difference of slopes and survives the objection that motivated
this check.

Lexical Concreteness is the one to downgrade. Its non-STEM
relationship reverses direction inside the common support --
falling to a minimum near the middle of the range and rising
after it -- so the positive linear coefficient summarises a shape
rather than describing one, and the cross-domain sign reversal is
a reversal of that summary. Section 4.2 now states it as a
difference in the linear approximation, and the reversal is no
longer offered as the paper's most interesting qualitative
finding.

noisier than difficulty estimated from a thousand. Two disjoint
random cohorts of 99 models each, drawn without regard to
generation, agree at:

  same-size random-cohort ceiling        : r = 0.9672
  early against recent                   : r = 0.8317   (86% of the ceiling)

A contaminated cohort should find leaked items easy that an
uncontaminated one finds hard, which would depress the observed
correlation relative to that ceiling. It does not depress it much.

PART 3: BOTH AT ONCE

The strictest available pool: flagged items removed, difficulty
estimated on the early cohort alone.

  N = 13668   STEM R2 0.0751   non-STEM R2 0.0552
  W(9) = 126.57, p = 6.02e-23


READING

Label error is real and measurable at 6.2% of the re-annotated
items, and flagged items are systematically harder, exactly as a
wrong key predicts. It is not, however, what produces the domain
contrast: dropping every flagged item leaves both partition fits
and the joint test essentially where they were, and refitting on
the verified subset alone -- a fortieth of the original pool, with
the loss of power that implies -- does not reverse the ordering.

Contamination is likewise not the mechanism. Difficulty
estimated on models from the Pythia and Llama-2 era correlates
at r = 0.832 with difficulty estimated on the 2024
generation, against a same-size sampling ceiling of 0.967,
and the domain contrast appears at similar magnitude
in both cohorts with contrast vectors correlated at 0.98. If
memorisation prevalence were driving the structure, the two
cohorts would not agree this closely about which items are hard.

WSCG CONVERGENT VALIDITY

Correlations between the shipped WSCG indicators and an externally
annotated measure of reasoning demand, with question length as the
competing explanation.

--- GSM8K (N = 1301), criterion: annotated solution steps ---

Predictor              Pearson r           p   Spearman
-------------------------------------------------------
WSCG Depth                0.2182    1.71e-15     0.2145
WSCG Nodes                0.3904    1.29e-48     0.3860
Word count                0.3636    6.21e-42     0.3509

  Partial r(WSCG Depth, criterion | word count) = 0.0864 (p = 1.82e-03)
  Partial r(WSCG Nodes, criterion | word count) = 0.1550 (p = 1.92e-08)
  R2, word count only:            0.1322
  R2, word count + WSCG:          0.1534

--- MATH (N = 1187), criterion: human difficulty level ---

Predictor              Pearson r           p   Spearman
-------------------------------------------------------
WSCG Depth                0.1463    4.11e-07     0.1631
WSCG Nodes                0.2068    6.20e-13     0.3364
Word count                0.2946    3.41e-25     0.3297

  Partial r(WSCG Depth, criterion | word count) = 0.0219 (p = 4.51e-01)
  Partial r(WSCG Nodes, criterion | word count) = -0.0128 (p = 6.59e-01)
  R2, word count only:            0.0868
  R2, word count + WSCG:          0.0891


READING

The result is positive on one criterion and null on the other, and the
difference between them is interpretable rather than awkward.

Against GSM8K's annotated solution steps, WSCG Nodes correlates at 0.390
and outperforms word count (0.364). More to the point, both indicators
retain an independent association once length is held fixed (partial r =
0.155 for nodes, p = 1.9e-08; 0.086 for depth, p = 1.8e-03), and adding
them to a length-only model raises R2 from 0.1322 to 0.1534. The criterion
was produced by that dataset's annotators for their own purposes, is not
derivable from the question text, and was not consulted when the weights
were set. That is convergent validity, and it is incremental over length.

Against MATH's human difficulty levels it is not. Word count predicts the
level better than either WSCG indicator (0.295 against 0.207 and 0.146),
and neither retains a partial association once length is controlled
(0.022 and -0.013, both n.s.). We report this rather than omitting it.

The two criteria are not measuring the same thing. GSM8K step counts are a
direct tally of sequential inferential operations, which is what the WSCG
is built to capture. A MATH difficulty level is a holistic judgment that
folds in advanced content knowledge, notation and problem novelty, most of
which the WSCG does not claim to measure and none of which it reads. The
honest statement is therefore narrow: the WSCG tracks the number of
reasoning steps an item demands, beyond how long the item is, and does not
track overall perceived difficulty. That is the construct the paper claims
for it, and the boundary is worth stating explicitly.

Effect sizes are modest throughout, and this is convergent validity for the
indicator rather than calibration of the weight tiers. The interval spacing
of the tiers remains where the sensitivity analysis leaves it: unvalidated
in magnitude, immaterial to the conclusions across 123 perturbation schemes.
